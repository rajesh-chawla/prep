Use this as your simple ownership-risk checklist. The goal is not to eliminate risk; it is to make early risks **cheap, reversible, and evidence-driven**.

```markdown
# Owner Path: Simple Risk Checklist

## Core question

Before making a major commitment, prove:

1. Someone has urgent pain.
2. A buyer will pay to solve it.
3. I can deliver it safely and profitably.
4. The solution repeats across customers.
5. I can afford the time and downside required to learn.

## The five risks

### 1. Market

**Risk:** The idea is interesting, but not urgent enough to buy.

**Test:**
- Talk to 10–20 target buyers.
- Ask about the last time the workflow failed.
- Identify the budget owner.
- Ask for a paid discovery or pilot.

**Good signal:** A buyer shares process/data, introduces a budget owner, or agrees to pay.

---

### 2. Product

**Risk:** Every customer needs a custom system.

**Test:**
- Define one narrow workflow.
- Define what is explicitly out of scope.
- Compare the first three customer requests.

**Rule:** Do not build a general “agent workflow platform” first.

**Good signal:** The same workflow, integrations, controls, and metrics repeat.

---

### 3. Delivery and trust

**Risk:** The system is too risky, unreliable, or difficult to deploy.

**Start with:**
- Read-only retrieval.
- Drafts and recommendations.
- Human approval before emails, record changes, or other side effects.
- Typed tools, identity, audit trail, and idempotency.

**Good signal:** A customer will allow a narrow pilot because the controls are credible.

---

### 4. Personal and financial

**Risk:** Runway, stress, or personal exposure force poor decisions.

**Decide in advance:**
- My financial loss limit is: ___
- My minimum runway is: ___ months
- My stop/re-evaluate date is: ___
- I will not take personal debt/guarantees beyond: ___

**Good signal:** You can test the idea without putting household stability at risk.

---

### 5. Legal and ownership

**Risk:** Employment agreements, IP, data, or conflicts make the opportunity unsafe.

**Before commercializing:**
- Review employment/invention-assignment terms.
- Keep work, code, data, hardware, and time completely separate.
- Keep dated records of independent work.
- Get legal advice before signing customers, raising money, or making a related employment move.

**Rule:** If IP ownership is unclear, stop and resolve it first.

## Evidence ladder

Do not confuse interest with demand.

1. “Interesting” → no proof.
2. Detailed pain story → problem proof.
3. Buyer shares process/data → trust and urgency.
4. Buyer introduces budget/security/IT → serious.
5. Paid discovery → commercial proof.
6. Paid pilot → strong proof.
7. Renewal/expansion/referral → repeatability.
8. Several customers buy the same offer → business.

## My recommended path

1. Keep or secure stable income.
2. Pick one narrow customer problem.
3. Have 10–20 discovery conversations.
4. Sell paid discovery or a fixed-scope pilot before building broadly.
5. Track what repeats.
6. Productize only the repeated core.
7. Increase commitment only after evidence, not excitement.

## One rule

> Do not take irreversible risk to answer a question that a customer conversation, paid discovery project, or narrow pilot can answer cheaply.
```

## One-sentence version

> Start with a narrow paid problem, prove repeatable demand, keep early risk reversible, protect financial and legal boundaries, and only increase commitment when customer evidence—not technical excitement—supports it.
